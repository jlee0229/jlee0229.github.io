Cosets vs irreps

What 89 small networks did with group multiplication, and what I got wrong along the way.

September 2026 · Pre-registered, except where marked post hoc

Give a small neural network the multiplication table of a group, hide some of the entries, and train it until it fills in the rest. Somewhere inside, the network has found a way to multiply. I wanted to know what that way looks like.

There are two published answers, and they disagree. Chughtai et al. argue that the neurons line up with the group's irreducible representations, or irreps.1 Stander et al. argue that they line up with cosets of subgroups, a much more elementary object: pick a subgroup, and a neuron responds according to which coset the input falls in.

Both papers study the same kind of model. So which one is right?

Before training anything, I wrote down what each answer predicts and froze those predictions in pre-registration files.2 Then I trained 89 networks.

The setup

Every model is a one-hidden-layer ReLU MLP of width 128, trained with one of two optimizers. The groups are:

  • the symmetric group S5, with 50 models,
  • the dihedral groups D59 and D61, with 8,
  • the affine groups Aff(ℤn) for n = 11, 15, 16 and 21, with 31.

The affine groups are the ones I know best. Sergiy Koshkin and I worked out their conjugacy classes and representations, so I knew what their irreps look like before I looked at a single neuron.

The answer: cosets

On S5, it wasn't close. All 50 models put at least 90% of their pre-activation energy in coset-aligned neurons, with a median of 100%. Untrained networks score 0. On the affine groups, 3 of the 4 blocks I could test passed as plain cosets, and the fourth (n = 21) was unclear.

What surprised me more is what the networks didn't do. The single-frequency irrep basis solves the task with less redundancy, so a network that wanted to be efficient would use it. It never carried a majority in any block. The networks had the cheaper option and passed on it.

The networks also took groups apart. Aff(ℤ15) and Aff(ℤ21) split into smaller groups, since 15 = 3 · 5 and 21 = 3 · 7, and every model solved the pieces separately and left the joint block empty.

Why does width matter? (post hoc)

Here I went off script, so treat this section as a lead rather than a result. If the network builds cosets, I can build the same circuit by hand and count its neurons. On Aff(𝔽p) the hand-built coset circuit needs about p2 neurons, and that is roughly where grokking starts. At p = 11, none of 3 seeds grok at width 100 and all 3 do at width 140. At p = 13, all 6 runs fail at width 128, and every run at width 160 or above succeeds.

I also swapped the activation. With |t| instead of ReLU, the networks build the same coset circuit. With a quadratic activation the coset preference disappears, but the networks don't switch to the irrep basis either. So the answer to "cosets or irreps?" seems to depend on the activation, which neither paper set out to test.

What I got wrong

Aff(ℤ13) never learned at width 128, which the width results above now explain. The memorizer control I used for S5 was the wrong null, and I recorded it as a design error rather than quietly fixing it. Two of the pre-registered predictions for Aff(ℤ15) came out inconclusive.

There is also a gap in the paper trail. I pushed the repository as one commit after the work was done, so git history can't show when I froze each pre-registration. The only record of the order is the date written inside each file.

And the claim itself is narrow. Every model here is a one-hidden-layer MLP at width 128. In this setting, networks prefer cosets. Whether that holds for deeper networks or transformers, I haven't tested.
