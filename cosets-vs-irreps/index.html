Cosets vs irreps

What 89 small networks did with group multiplication.

September 2026 · Pre-registered, except where marked post hoc

Train a small neural network on the multiplication table of a group, with some of the entries hidden, and it can learn to fill in the rest. I wanted to know how it does that.

There are two published answers, and they disagree. Chughtai et al. found that the neurons line up with the group's irreducible representations, or irreps.1 Stander et al. found that they line up with cosets of subgroups: pick a subgroup, and a neuron responds according to which coset the input falls in.

Both papers study the same kind of model, so which one is right?

Before training anything, I wrote down what each answer predicts and froze those predictions in pre-registration files.2 Then I trained 89 networks.

The setup

Every model is a one-hidden-layer ReLU MLP of width 128, trained with one of two optimizers. The groups are:

  • the symmetric group S5, with 50 models,
  • the dihedral groups D59 and D61, with 8,
  • the affine groups Aff(ℤn) for n = 11, 15, 16 and 21, with 31.

The affine groups are the ones I know best. Sergiy Koshkin and I worked out their conjugacy classes and representations, so I knew what their irreps look like before I looked at a single neuron.

The answer: cosets

On S5, all 50 models put at least 90% of their pre-activation energy in coset-aligned neurons, with a median of 100%. Untrained networks score 0. On the affine groups, 3 of the 4 blocks I could test passed as plain cosets, and the fourth (n = 21) was unclear.

The single-frequency irrep basis never carried a majority in any block, even though it solves the task with less redundancy.

The networks also split groups into pieces when they could. Aff(ℤ15) and Aff(ℤ21) break into smaller groups (15 = 3 · 5 and 21 = 3 · 7), and every model solved the pieces separately and left the joint block empty.

Width (post hoc)

I didn't pre-register this part, so I'd treat it as a lead. If the network builds cosets, I can build the same circuit by hand and count its neurons. On Aff(𝔽p), the hand-built coset circuit needs about p2 neurons, and grokking starts near that width. At p = 11, none of 3 seeds grok at width 100 and all 3 do at width 140. At p = 13, all 6 runs fail at width 128, and every run at width 160 or above succeeds.

I also tried other activations, again post hoc. With |t| instead of ReLU, the networks build the same coset circuit. With a quadratic activation the coset preference disappears, and the networks don't switch to the irrep basis either.

What didn't work

Aff(ℤ13) never learned at width 128. That fits the width results, since 132 = 169. The memorizer control I used for S5 was the wrong null, and I recorded it as a design error. Two of the pre-registered predictions for Aff(ℤ15) came out inconclusive.

I also pushed the repository as one commit after the work was done, so git history can't show when I froze each pre-registration. The only record of the order is the date written inside each file.

Every pre-registered model is a one-hidden-layer MLP of width 128, so the coset result is a claim about that setting. I haven't tested deeper networks or transformers.
